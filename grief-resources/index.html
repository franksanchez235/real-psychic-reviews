Grief Resources

Healing after loss takes time, support, and self-compassion. We have gathered resources to support you alongside your medium reading journey.

Understanding Grief

Grief is not a linear process. It does not follow a tidy timeline, and it does not look the same for any two people. You may feel fine one day and overwhelmed the next. You may experience anger, guilt, profound sadness, or even numbness. All of these responses are normal.

The most important thing to understand about grief is that there is no wrong way to experience it. Your grief is as unique as your relationship with the person you have lost.

When a Medium Reading Can Help

A medium reading is not a substitute for grief counselling or therapy. It is a complementary experience that many bereaved individuals find profoundly comforting. A reading can help when:

  • You feel a persistent need to know that your loved one is at peace
  • There were words left unspoken that weigh on your heart
  • You are struggling with the finality of physical death
  • You want reassurance that your loved one remains aware of your life
  • You feel stuck in your grief and are looking for a catalyst for healing

Self-Care During Grief

Allow Yourself to Feel

Suppressing emotions delays healing. Give yourself permission to cry, to be angry, to feel lost. These are not signs of weakness — they are signs of love.

Maintain Basic Routines

Grief can make even simple tasks feel monumental. Try to maintain basic routines — eating, sleeping, moving your body — even when motivation is low.

Accept Help

When people offer support, let them help. Grief is not meant to be carried alone.

Be Patient With Yourself

There is no deadline for healing. Grief does not expire after a year, or two, or five. Give yourself the time you need.

Professional Support

If your grief feels unmanageable, please reach out to professional support:

  • Your GP or doctor can provide referrals
  • Bereavement counselling services are available locally and online
  • Support groups connect you with others who understand
  • Crisis helplines are available 24/7 for immediate support

Finding Support That Fits Your Situation

Grief support comes in many forms, and what helps one person may not help another. This section outlines common options so you can decide what suits you. It is general information, not medical advice.

Types of support

  • One-to-one counselling: a trained therapist offers a private space to talk, sometimes with specific approaches for bereavement.
  • Peer support groups: meeting people who have faced similar losses can ease isolation.
  • Helplines and online chat: useful at night or when you want to talk without arranging an appointment.
  • Faith or community leaders: some find comfort in rituals and shared mourning.
  • Books and written resources: reading others’ accounts can help put words to feelings.

Recognising When Grief Needs More Help

Intense sorrow is a normal response to loss. It is worth speaking to a doctor or counsellor if weeks or months pass and you cannot function at work or home, if you stop eating or sleeping, if you feel numb to everything, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself. If you are ever in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number straight away.

Supporting Someone Who Is Grieving

  • Offer specific help, such as cooking a meal or running an errand, rather than “let me know if you need anything”.
  • Say the person’s name and share memories if the griever wants that.
  • Avoid saying that everything happens for a reason or comparing losses.
  • Keep checking in after the first weeks, when most visitors have gone.

Thinking Critically About Spiritual Services While Grieving

Bereavement can leave anyone more vulnerable to persuasive promises. If you are considering a psychic or medium, set a budget beforehand, avoid paying for repeated sessions out of urgency, and be wary of anyone who claims your loved one is troubled unless you pay. Practical Tasks That Can Wait

After a loss there are administrative demands: registering the death, managing accounts, dealing with property. Where possible, take them one at a time and ask relatives or an adviser for help. Large financial or housing decisions are better delayed if you can manage it.

Frequently asked questions

How long does grief last?

There is no fixed timeline. Many people find the intensity eases over time, but waves can return around anniversaries or life events.

Is it wrong to seek comfort from a medium?

Many people find it comforting, and that is a personal choice. It works best alongside, not instead of, support from people and professionals you trust.

Where can I find a counsellor?

Your doctor can usually advise, and national bereavement charities and health services list accredited counsellors and helplines in most countries.
